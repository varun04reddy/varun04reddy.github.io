What Linear Probes Actually Measure

Created in April 21, 2026

2026   ·   technical  

There is a running joke in ML that every interpretability technique is either a probe or an attention visualization. The joke is mostly true, and the probe half is the more interesting half to think about carefully.

Linear probes—a frozen linear classifier or regressor trained on top of a model’s internal activations to predict some target variable—have been used in computational neuroscience since at least the early 1990s under the name population decoding, and in deep learning interpretability since Alain and Bengio formalized them in 2016. Both fields rediscovered the same tool independently, developed similar enthusiasm for it, ran into similar critiques around what it actually establishes, and have spent years working out a careful epistemology around its limits. That parallel trajectory is, I think, more informative than either field’s conclusions on their own.

My view is that linear probes keep working not because they are a good approximation to richer analysis, but because linear accessibility is itself a meaningful property of learned systems. When a network needs to use a variable at some layer, gradient descent tends to leave that variable linearly readable at the input of that layer—it is the format that costs the least to route downstream. So probes, when properly controlled, are measuring something real about how the network has organized its representations. What they are not measuring is whether the variable is causally used, whether the representation is geometrically simple, or whether the model will behave consistently in situations the probe wasn’t designed to test. Those are different questions, and answering them requires different tools.

This post tries to be precise about that distinction. It covers the neuroscience version of the problem, the deep learning version, where the two converge and where they differ, the history of interpretability tools that have tried to go beyond probes, and why the scaling properties of those richer tools are now the dominant practical constraint on what mechanistic interpretability can actually do at the frontier.

Three Questions That Usually Get Conflated

Before anything else, it helps to precisely separate three questions that a successful linear probe is often assumed to answer simultaneously.

Question A: Is the variable decodable? Can an affine map from the hidden state \(h \in \mathbb{R}^d\) recover the target variable \(y\) with low error? For continuous targets this means small mean squared error under a linear model \(\hat{y} = w^T h + b\); for discrete ones it means high accuracy of a linear classifier. This is what a probe measures, directly and unambiguously.

Question B: Is the variable causally used? Does the system actually rely on this variable when producing its behavior? If you intervene on the representation—clamp the direction the probe identifies, ablate it, patch it from a different input context—does behavior change in the way you would predict?

Question C: Is the variable represented simply? Is the concept stored as a clean direction in the ambient space, a single geometric axis you could point to? Or is the probe succeeding while the underlying structure is curved, distributed across a subspace, or entangled with other variables in ways the probe’s scalar accuracy hides?

A linear probe answers Question A by construction. It makes no direct contact with B or C. The history of confusion around probing—in both neuroscience and interpretability—is largely the history of treating a positive answer to A as implicit evidence for B and C without carefully accounting for the gaps. Getting those gaps right determines what probes are useful for and where you need different tools.

The Neuroscience Background: Population Decoding and Its Limits

Computational neuroscience faced a hard version of the decoding problem before any of the interpretability literature existed. Neural population recordings are noisy, high-dimensional, and only partially observed. You cannot read synaptic weights. You cannot intervene cleanly on specified internal states. The question “what does this brain area represent?” must be answered from correlational data alone, which makes precise language about what correlation-based methods do and do not establish essential rather than optional.

The dominant framework that emerged is the encoding-decoding distinction. An encoding model predicts neural population activity from the stimulus or task variable: the goal is to find \(f_\theta\) such that \(\hat{r} = f_\theta(\text{stimulus})\) approximates the population response \(r \in \mathbb{R}^N\) well. A decoding model goes in the opposite direction, recovering the stimulus from the activity: find \(g\) such that \(\hat{\theta} = g(r)\) approximates the true stimulus. The two are related—if a sufficiently accurate encoding model exists, some decoding is usually possible by inverting it—but they ask different questions. Encoding models tell you how the stimulus projects onto the population basis; decoding models tell you how much stimulus information a downstream reader could extract. These are not the same.

The theoretical grounding for linear decoding in neuroscience comes from the Fisher information framework. For a population of \(N\) neurons with mean response vector \(\mathbf{f}(\theta)\) given stimulus \(\theta\), and noise covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\theta)\), the Fisher information about \(\theta\) is:

\[I_F(\theta) = \mathbf{f}'(\theta)^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\theta)\, \mathbf{f}'(\theta)\]

where \(\mathbf{f}'(\theta) = \partial \mathbf{f}/\partial \theta\) is the derivative of the tuning curve vector with respect to the stimulus. The Cramér-Rao bound says that any unbiased estimator \(\hat{\theta}\) must satisfy:

\[\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \geq \frac{1}{I_F(\theta)}\]

The optimal linear estimator achieves:

\[\hat{\theta}_{\text{linear}} = \mathbf{w}^T r, \quad \mathbf{w}_{\text{opt}} \propto \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{f}'(\theta)\]

which in practice is estimated by ridge regression or cross-validated ordinary least squares from recorded stimulus-response pairs. The critical quantity is the linear Fisher information \(I_{LF}\), defined as \(1/\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{\text{linear}})\). For Gaussian noise, \(I_{LF} = I_F\) and the linear estimator is optimal. For non-Gaussian or non-stationary noise, \(I_{LF} \leq I_F\): the linear decoder leaves some stimulus information on the table because it cannot exploit higher-order statistical structure.

This framework gives neuroscientists a precise calibration for how much information is available via linear decoding versus how much exists in principle. It made linear decoding not just a heuristic, but a theoretically grounded lower bound on representational content.

A deeper result came from Moreno-Bote et al. (2014), who identified a specific structure of noise correlations—called differential correlations—that fundamentally limits what any linear decoder can extract. Differential correlations are noise fluctuations that are aligned with the direction \(\mathbf{f}'(\theta)\), the direction in population space along which the stimulus signal lies. The critical implication is that a linear decoder cannot distinguish signal from noise when the two share the same direction. No matter how many neurons you record simultaneously, adding more does not help recover the information that differential correlations mask, because those correlations scale with population size. This was a major corrective to naive intuitions about large neural populations: more neurons means more Fisher information only when the relevant noise correlations are benign.

What this means practically: linear decoding accuracy from a neural population tells you how much information is available to a linear downstream reader, accounting for the noise structure the population actually has. It is information measured at the interface between systems, not information about the absolute representational content of a single area. This distinction maps cleanly onto the LLM probe setting, as we will see.

Probes in Deep Learning: From Diagnostic to Debate

The design is simple: freeze the pretrained network, extract hidden activations \(h_l\) at each layer \(l\), and train a separate linear classifier \(g_l: h_l \to y\) on a held-out task label \(y\). The accuracy \(\text{acc}(g_l)\) at each layer profiles which layer’s representations are already organized to make \(y\) linearly accessible. The framing was deliberately modest—probes as a diagnostic for how suitable an intermediate representation is, not as a claim about mechanism.

Probe accuracy tends to increase monotonically with layer depth on classification tasks—deeper representations are more task-relevant. More usefully, the profile across layers differs substantially for different target variables. This makes probing informative even when you have no hypothesis about where in the network a variable should be encoded: you run the sweep and the data tells you.

When the method expanded into NLP and we started probing BERT and related models systematically, a clear hierarchical picture emerged: surface features and part-of-speech tags are most accessible in lower layers, syntactic dependencies and named entities in middle layers, coreference and semantic roles in higher layers. This matched theoretical expectations and was reproducible across runs and datasets.

We can push probing further than label prediction. Rather than asking whether a categorical variable is linearly separable, we can ask whether the full geometric structure of a linguistic object is encoded. A structural probe does this for syntax trees: we learn a linear transformation \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}\) of the word representations such that:

\[d_B(h_i, h_j)^2 = \|B(h_i - h_j)\|_2^2 \approx d_{\text{tree}}(w_i, w_j)\]

where \(d_{\text{tree}}(w_i, w_j)\) is the number of edges in the dependency parse tree between words \(i\) and \(j\). We can separately probe whether tree depth is encoded in the norms:

\[\text{depth}_B(h_i) = \|B h_i\|_2 \approx \text{depth}_{\text{tree}}(w_i)\]

For BERT, Spearman correlation between predicted and true tree distances exceeds 0.85 in middle layers. When this works it says something stronger than label decodability: the full metric geometry of syntactic structure is preserved under a learned linear map. This is a compact formulation of what it would mean for a concept to be linearly represented in a rich, structural sense, not just in the crude “class-separable” one.

Then the skepticism arrived, and it was correct.

The Backlash: What Probe Accuracy Conflates

The decisive critique is this: probe accuracy conflates two things that should be separated—how much information is present in the representation, and how much the probe’s own parameters can learn from scratch.

The argument is straightforward. Consider a probe \(g: h_l \to y\) trained on layer-\(l\) representations. Even if \(h_l\) contains no task-relevant information whatsoever, a sufficiently parameterized probe could still achieve above-chance accuracy by memorizing statistical regularities between hidden states and labels that happen to exist in the probe’s training data. To separate these contributions, we can use control tasks: random but fixed mappings \(\tilde{y}(x) = \text{Random}(x)\) that assign arbitrary labels to word types. Because the mapping is random, no representation \(h_l\) can contain useful information for it—any above-chance accuracy on the control task reflects the probe’s memorization capacity, not the representation’s structure.

The key measure is selectivity:

\[\text{Selectivity}(l) = \text{acc}(g_l^{\text{linguistic}}) - \text{acc}(g_l^{\text{control}})\]

where the two probes have identical architecture and training procedure, one on real labels and one on random labels. A high-selectivity probe is learning from the representation; a low-selectivity probe is learning from its own parameters. The uncomfortable result: complex probes like deep MLPs have low selectivity—they achieve similar accuracy on linguistic and control tasks, meaning most of their reported accuracy is probe capacity rather than representational content. Linear probes and small MLPs achieve higher selectivity. Dropout, counterintuitively, does not help; small architecture is what actually matters.

This finding has an immediate implication for probe interpretation. A linear probe achieving 85% accuracy on a POS tagging task, with a control-task accuracy of 65% on the same layer, tells you something real: the representation genuinely contributes 20 percentage points of accuracy beyond what the probe could produce by memorization. A nonlinear probe achieving 90% accuracy with a control-task accuracy of 88% is telling you almost nothing about the representation—it is a self-report on the probe’s capacity.

The second critique is older but equally important: probe accuracy is a scalar that hides the distribution of errors. 85% accuracy means 15% of examples are wrong, and those errors are almost certainly not random. They cluster by context, input type, syntactic environment, token frequency, and a dozen other variables that the scalar discards. In neuroscience, the analogous point is that a decoder’s average decoding accuracy across stimuli conceals stimulus-specific decoding variance that may be scientifically important—the brain area may represent some stimulus configurations excellently and others poorly, and the average hides the structure.

Why Linear Accessibility Is Not a Trivial Property

The probe skepticism literature, when read carefully, does not say that probe success is meaningless. It says that probe accuracy requires control and careful interpretation. Once you have applied the controls, a high-selectivity probe result is genuinely informative.

The theoretical argument for why rests on how learned systems pass information between their components. A network processing input \(x\) through successive layers computes:

\[h_{l+1} = \phi(W_l h_l + b_l)\]

where \(\phi\) is a pointwise nonlinearity. Any downstream layer \(l' > l\) can in principle apply complex nonlinear operations to extract arbitrary functions of \(h_l\). But the simplest computation—the one requiring the fewest additional parameters and the least additional nonlinear processing—is a direct linear readout. If a useful variable \(y\) is linearly accessible in \(h_l\), the network can route it to wherever it needs to be used with a minimal weight vector. If \(y\) is encoded nonlinearly—recoverable only through several additional nonlinear transformations—using it requires proportionally more computational machinery.

This creates an optimization pressure argument. If the training objective requires the network to use variable \(y\) at some downstream layer, gradient descent will tend to produce configurations where \(y\) is linearly accessible at the input of that layer. The reason is that linear accessibility minimizes the representational preprocessing cost: the network allocates to maintaining \(y\) in a format that does not require additional extraction. This is the core of the linear representation hypothesis: high-level concepts in learned networks tend to be linearly encoded as directions, because directions are the format that requires zero additional computation to use downstream.

Formally, Park et al. define a concept \(C\) as linearly represented in \(\mathcal{H}\) if there exists a direction \(v_C \in \mathcal{H}\) and a functional \(\phi_C: \mathcal{H} \to \mathbb{R}\) such that:

\[\phi_C(h) = \langle h, v_C \rangle_M\]

predicts the degree of \(C\) in the input corresponding to \(h\), where \(\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_M\) is the inner product in the representation’s natural metric \(M\). The subtlety is the metric. The Euclidean inner product on the raw activation space may not be the right geometry. Park et al. show that the representation geometry that best supports linear representation may be non-Euclidean—the “correct” inner product that makes concepts linearly accessible is learned, and naively using Euclidean distance can make linearly represented concepts appear nonlinearly encoded.

The Gap Between Probe Accuracy and Understanding

Despite the theoretical motivation for expecting linear accessibility, probe accuracy conceals several distinct failure modes.

Unexplained variance. A classification probe reporting 85% accuracy has 15% structured error, and that structured residual is often where the most interesting science is. In neuroscience, the residual of a linear decoder—the variance in neural activity not explained by the fitted tuning model—has been a productive object of study in its own right. It contains contributions from attention, behavioral state, arousal, and variables the experimenter did not think to measure. In interpretability, the probe’s error distribution across contexts, token types, and distributional corners reveals where the linear representation assumption breaks down. A probe that achieves high accuracy in-distribution but whose errors cluster at rare or semantically unusual inputs is pointing directly at where the representation uses nonlinear or context-dependent structure.

For continuous targets it is worth tracking \(R^2\) rather than just classification accuracy:

\[R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i - \bar{y})^2}\]

A probe with \(R^2 = 0.7\) is leaving 30% of the variance in the target unexplained. Whether that 30% is random noise or structured signal is not readable from the scalar. Decomposing the residual \(y_i - \hat{y}_i\) by context type, token position, or semantic category is often more informative than the \(R^2\) itself.

Geometry mismatch. A linear probe’s success does not constrain the geometric form of the underlying representation. A clean example: a linear probe can accurately predict character count from language model activations, but analysis of those activations reveals the underlying representational structure is a helix—a curved one-dimensional manifold spiraling through a higher-dimensional subspace. The probe succeeds because a linear direction cutting through the helix at many turns achieves reasonable average decoding accuracy. But the geometry is not a clean linear axis—if you try to intervene on character count by adding the probe direction to activations, you get unreliable results because natural variation in character count requires moving along the helix, not along a constant direction.

Concretely, a helical representation looks locally linear in any small neighborhood, but globally the probe direction intersects the helix at many distinct values of the variable. The relationship between the probe score \(\hat{y} = w^T h\) and the true character count has a high average correlation but substantial structured residual with a periodic pattern—exactly the signature of a curved manifold being approximated by a linear cut.

Multi-dimensional features. Some features in language models are irreducibly multi-dimensional: they require a subspace rather than a direction. The clearest examples are circular variables—things like day of week or position in an annual calendar that the network represents using two-dimensional embeddings corresponding to the sine and cosine of the circular angle. A single linear direction can only partially predict such a variable. Fitting the sine component gives an \(R^2\) bounded by roughly \(0.5\), even with perfect encoding, because half the variance lives orthogonally in the cosine component. A probe that reports 75% classification accuracy on days-of-week may look impressive while systematically confusing adjacent days through a mechanism the scalar cannot reveal.

The general statement: if a concept is encoded in a \(k\)-dimensional subspace for \(k > 1\), a standard one-dimensional linear probe finds the best one-dimensional projection of that subspace. The accuracy it reports is that one-dimensional performance. It is a lower bound on what a \(k\)-dimensional linear readout can achieve. The gap between the two (how much better you do with \(k\) directions than with one) is a measure of hidden dimensionality.

Causal ambiguity. This is the limitation both fields take most seriously. High probe accuracy is a purely correlational claim. A variable \(y\) that is perfectly decodable from \(h_l\) may be:

  1. Causally upstream of the model’s output computation,
  2. Computed incidentally as a byproduct of other operations,
  3. Present in the representation but never actually read out by downstream layers,
  4. Correlated with the true causal variable but not identical to it.

All four cases produce identical probe accuracy. Distinguishing them requires intervention: take an input where \(y\) has value \(v\), manually set the representation at layer \(l\) to correspond to value \(v'\) while keeping everything else fixed, and observe whether the output changes as predicted. This is activation patching (also called causal tracing), and it is the standard causal tool in interpretability. Importantly, a probe that fails on activation patching—where patching the probed direction does not change the model’s behavior—conclusively establishes that Question B has a different answer from Question A, regardless of how high the probe accuracy was.

The Interpretability Field’s Pragmatic Turn

Interpretability has gone through a recognizable oscillation. Early linear probe work was sometimes oversold as evidence of mechanistic understanding. Then came a skepticism phase arguing that probes are insufficiently mechanistic—a fair criticism in isolation. Then a period of heavier, more expensive methods—circuit analysis, sparse autoencoders—that promised to reveal more fundamental structure. Now the field has landed in a more explicitly pragmatic place: methods should be judged on whether they help with concrete tasks, not on whether they satisfy some philosophical standard of full mechanistic understanding.

Part of the renewed interest in probes is driven by safety requirements. When we train a probe on residual stream activations to predict whether a model is in a “helpful” versus “harmful” latent mode, and that probe reliably fires before the harmful behavior is visible in the model’s outputs, that is operationally useful. It does not solve Questions B or C. The probe might be reading a correlate of harmful intent rather than the causal variable itself, and the geometry of the harmful-intent direction may be more complex than a single axis. But for the purpose of building a scalable monitoring system, Question A being answered affirmatively is sufficient. A cheap, continuous monitor that provides reliable signal about a safety-relevant latent state is valuable independent of its mechanistic completeness.

More broadly, the community has started treating probe success as a screening condition rather than a conclusion. If a probe can’t find a variable, you don’t spend the compute on the richer analysis. If it can, and if it survives selectivity controls, you escalate. That workflow makes probes more defensible as a research tool than either the early boosterism or the later skepticism suggested.

What Neuroscience Knows That Interpretability Is Still Learning

The parallel histories of neuroscience decoding and interpretability probing are illuminating precisely because they exhibit the same developmental arc. What neuroscience offers interpretability is not a body of domain-specific knowledge but an epistemology—a carefully worked-out understanding of what correlational methods do and do not establish in complex learned systems.

The encoding-decoding distinction maps directly. In neuroscience, a brain area “encodes” a variable means that a good forward model \(\hat{r} = f(\theta)\) exists. A brain area “decodes” a variable means that \(\hat{\theta} = g(r)\) exists. These are formally different and can dissociate: an area might support excellent decoding of a variable it does not encode as a primary computational output, because the decoding exploits noise correlations or marginal distributions rather than the mean response. In interpretability terms: a probe succeeding at layer \(l\) does not mean layer \(l\) is where the variable is computed; it means layer \(l\)’s activations contain sufficient information for a linear reader to recover it. The variable might have been computed at layer \(l-5\) and propagated forward via residual connections without further processing.

The differential correlations result offers a direct analogy. Noise correlations aligned with the population’s signal direction set a hard ceiling on linear decoding, regardless of how many neurons are recorded. The analogous result in interpretability: correlated variation in the hidden state that is aligned with the probe direction but driven by unmeasured input variables other than \(y\) will inflate probe accuracy beyond the true contribution of \(y\) to the representation. If character co-occurrence patterns in training data cause the “character count” direction to correlate with other syntactic variables, a linear probe will report accuracy that conflates all of them. Selectivity controls mitigate but do not fully eliminate this confound.

What interpretability has that neuroscience never did is the ability to close the causal loop cheaply. In a two-hour experiment, you can:

  1. Run a linear probe to identify a candidate direction \(v\).
  2. Construct a control task to estimate selectivity.
  3. Run activation patching over a grid of inputs to test causal relevance.
  4. Fit a structural probe or low-rank subspace model to test for multi-dimensional encoding.
  5. Synthesize targeted counterfactual inputs to probe distributional robustness.

None of these require animal surgery, electrode implantation, or years of data collection. This means interpretability can move through the epistemological workflow that neuroscience spread across three decades in a single research sprint. The question is whether the field is organized to actually do so, rather than stopping after step 1.

[Experiment 1: Selectivity as a function of probe complexity. Setup: Choose a BERT-base model and a set of linguistic properties: POS tags, constituency span labels, named entity types, and a continuous variable like word frequency. For each property, train probes with varying complexity: logistic regression (linear), 1-hidden-layer MLP of width 64, 1-hidden-layer MLP of width 512, 2-hidden-layer MLP. For each complexity, compute both the task accuracy and the control-task accuracy using random word-type label assignments (Hewitt-Liang protocol). Compute selectivity as the difference.

Expected outcome: Selectivity should decrease monotonically as probe complexity increases. For the linear probe, selectivity should be highest, meaning the accuracy attributable to the representation (rather than probe memorization) is largest. For the 2-hidden-layer wide MLP, selectivity may approach zero, meaning most of the accuracy is the probe’s own capacity. The crossover point—where adding probe capacity stops improving selectivity—identifies the complexity level at which you are actually measuring the representation.

Why it matters: This establishes a principled calibration for probe complexity. Reports of probe accuracy without selectivity controls should be treated as uninformative. This experiment makes the control standard concrete and reproducible.]

[Experiment 2: Detecting multi-dimensional encoding with subspace probes. Setup: For a variable suspected of multi-dimensional encoding (e.g., day of week, month of year, or token position), compare: (a) a standard linear probe (1D projection), (b) a 2D probe that fits two orthogonal directions and reports both accuracy and \(R^2\) on a reconstruction of the full variable, (c) a circular probe that fits \(\sin(\theta)\) and \(\cos(\theta)\) where \(\theta\) is the circular variable.

Also compute the marginal improvement: define \(\Delta R^2(k) = R^2_k - R^2_{k-1}\) where \(R^2_k\) is the explained variance of the best \(k\)-dimensional linear probe. Plot \(\Delta R^2\) as a function of \(k\).

Expected outcome: For genuinely 1D linear representations, \(\Delta R^2\) drops rapidly after \(k=1\). For circular variables, \(\Delta R^2(2)\) should be close to \(\Delta R^2(1)\), with a sharp drop at \(k=3\). For complex manifolds, the decrease may be more gradual. The “elbow” in this curve identifies the intrinsic dimensionality of the encoding.

Why it matters: This directly tests the claim that some features require multi-dimensional representations. If day of week is genuinely encoded as a circular helix, the 1D probe should explain at most 50% of the variance and the 2D probe should capture near 100%. The profile of \(\Delta R^2(k)\) is a dimensionality fingerprint of the representation that is categorically more informative than probe accuracy alone.]

[Experiment 3: Probe accuracy vs. causal relevance under activation patching. Setup: Choose a model and task where grokking or factual recall can be tested (e.g., GPT-2 on the IOI task from the mechanistic interpretability literature). Train linear probes on residual stream activations at each layer \(l\) to predict the correct indirect object. For each layer, identify the top probe direction \(v_l\) and construct a patching experiment: run two forward passes, one with the correct prompt and one with a corrupt version, then patch the component of the residual stream along \(v_l\) from the clean run into the corrupt run at each layer separately. Measure how much the model’s probability on the correct token recovers as a function of which layer is patched.

Expected outcome: Layers where the probe has high accuracy and patching produces large recovery are causally relevant. Layers where the probe has high accuracy but patching produces small recovery have the information present but not used. The correlation between probe accuracy across layers and patching recovery across layers directly tests whether probe accuracy is a useful proxy for causal relevance.

Quantitative prediction: If probe accuracy were a perfect proxy for causal use, the layer-wise profile of probe accuracy and patching recovery would be identical. In practice they are expected to differ, because some early layers contain linearly decodable information that is not the causal driver of the output—the actual computation happens later, and residual connections carry the information forward passively.

Why it matters: This is the cleanest possible test of the gap between Question A and Question B. The experiment directly measures the correlation between linear accessibility and causal use across layers for the same task. A high correlation would validate probe-based hypotheses about mechanistic relevance; a low one would establish that probes are a poor guide to causal structure.]

A Recommended Methodological Stack

Given all of the above, a sensible methodology treats probes not as standalone evidence but as the first step in a staged workflow:

Step 1—Probe for linear accessibility. Train a well-controlled linear probe with selectivity scored against a control task. Identify which layers and which candidate directions have high, selective accuracy. This costs essentially nothing computationally and screens for whether a deeper investigation is worthwhile.

Step 2—Analyze the residual. Do not stop at the accuracy scalar. Decompose the probe’s error by input type, context, token class, and distributional bin. Identify where accuracy is low and where it degrades under distribution shift. This tells you whether the linear representation is uniform or whether it has holes.

Step 3—Test causal relevance. Run activation patching on the candidate directions at the candidate layers. This directly tests Question B. If patching the probe direction does not change behavior, you are looking at a correlate rather than a cause.

Step 4—Characterize representational geometry. Use subspace probes, structural probes, or manifold fitting to test Question C. Does the representation live on a 1D line, a 2D subspace, or a curved manifold? The \(\Delta R^2(k)\) profile from Experiment 2 above is one concrete operationalization.

Step 5—Escalate if justified. SAE decomposition, full circuit analysis, and mechanistic tracing are expensive. They become justified once Step 3 has established causal relevance and Step 4 has revealed that the representational geometry is complex enough to require them.

This stack respects the practical reality that interpretability resources are finite and that most candidate variables either will not survive Step 1 (not linearly accessible), Step 2 (only narrowly accessible), or Step 3 (not causally used). Only the ones that survive all three are worth the investment of deeper analysis.

A Brief History of Mechanistic Interpretability Tools

To understand where probes sit today, it helps to have the broader picture of how the interpretability field arrived at its current toolkit. The story is roughly one of escalating ambition followed by escalating computational cost, with the field now facing a scaling wall that is forcing a reassessment of what is actually tractable.

The early period was dominated by attention visualization and neuron analysis. The operating assumption was that individual attention heads or neurons could be mapped to human-interpretable functions—this head attends to previous tokens, this neuron fires for vowels. These approaches produced real insights on small models; the induction head circuit in small transformers is a clean and well-validated example. But they broke down at scale for a fundamental reason: individual neurons in large models are overwhelmingly polysemantic. A single neuron responds to dozens of unrelated features simultaneously, making one-to-one interpretation impossible even in principle.

The theoretical explanation came from the superposition hypothesis (Elhage et al., 2022). A network with \(d\) dimensions and \(n \gg d\) relevant features can represent all \(n\) by packing them as approximately orthogonal directions in the \(d\)-dimensional space, exploiting feature sparsity. If feature \(i\) has direction \(v_i\) and features activate independently with sparsity \(s\), the expected interference from superposition when feature \(i\) is active is:

\[\text{interference} \approx (n - 1)(1-s)\,\mathbb{E}\left[(v_i^T v_j)^2\right] \approx \frac{(n-1)(1-s)}{d}\]

For large \(d\) and sparse \(s\), this is tolerable. The network accepts a small reconstruction cost in exchange for representing vastly more features than it has dimensions. The consequence for interpretability is immediate: individual neurons are not the right unit of analysis—the representational atoms are directions in activation space, not basis-aligned components.

This motivated sparse autoencoders (SAEs), which have become the dominant feature-discovery tool in mechanistic interpretability. You train an autoencoder with a sparsity penalty on the hidden layer to reconstruct activations from a target network layer. The sparse hidden units are hypothesized to correspond to monosemantic features—concepts that each hidden unit encodes cleanly, without the polysemanticity of the raw neurons. Formally, for activation \(h \in \mathbb{R}^d\), the SAE computes:

\[\hat{h} = W_{\text{dec}}\,\sigma(W_{\text{enc}}\,h + b_{\text{enc}}) + b_{\text{dec}}\]

trained to minimize:

\[\mathcal{L} = \|h - \hat{h}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\sigma(W_{\text{enc}}\,h + b_{\text{enc}})\|_1\]

The \(\ell_1\) penalty drives most hidden units to zero for any given input, encouraging each unit to activate for only one or a few semantically coherent features. Anthropic’s SAE work on Claude found features corresponding to specific semantic concepts, emotions, and reasoning patterns that were invisible in the raw neuron basis—strong evidence that the superposition picture is real and that SAE-style decomposition can partially undo it.

The limitation of SAEs is that they operate one layer at a time. Circuits in transformers span multiple layers: a feature computed at layer 10 may depend on attention heads at layers 5 and 8 that themselves combine features from earlier layers. Transcoders address the MLP sublayers specifically. Rather than reconstructing a layer’s output from itself, a transcoder learns to approximate the MLP’s input-to-output computation in a sparse, interpretable form. If the MLP computes \(\text{MLP}(h) = f(h)\), the transcoder learns \(\tilde{f}(h) \approx f(h)\) using a sparse feature basis. This lets you replace an opaque MLP with an approximately equivalent interpretable one and trace how specific input features cause specific output changes.

Crosscoders and cross-layer transcoders (CLTs) generalize further still. A crosscoder can read and write at multiple layers simultaneously, tracking a feature as it persists, transforms, or combines across the full depth of the network. Many features in transformer residual streams are stable across many layers—they propagate via residual connections without modification. Per-layer SAEs miss this structure because they fit separate dictionaries at each layer and must then reconcile which layer-\(l\) features correspond to which layer-\((l+1)\) features as a post-hoc analysis. CLTs define features globally across layers, identifying directly which layers modify them and how.

These tools represent a real technical achievement. They have revealed structure invisible in the neuron basis, enabled partial circuit analysis of nontrivial behaviors, and produced a vocabulary for discussing model internals with more mechanistic grounding than correlation-based approaches can provide. But the cost of applying them is now the central bottleneck.

The Scaling Wall

SAEs, transcoders, and crosscoders were developed and validated primarily on small to medium models—GPT-2, the Pythia family up to a few billion parameters, smaller Claude variants. Applying them to frontier models is a qualitatively different problem.

The compute for training SAEs at scale grows roughly as:

\[\text{cost} \sim L \cdot d_{\text{SAE}}^2 \cdot T_{\text{tokens}}\]

where \(L\) is the number of layers, \(d_{\text{SAE}}\) is the SAE dictionary size (typically \(16\times\) to \(64\times\) the model’s hidden dimension), and \(T_{\text{tokens}}\) is the number of activation samples. For a 70B parameter model with hidden dimension \(d = 8192\), \(L = 80\) layers, dictionary size \(d_{\text{SAE}} = 131072\), and \(T = 10^9\) tokens, this is on the order of tens of petaFLOPs of training compute—comparable to training a medium-sized language model from scratch, to produce a feature dictionary of a single existing one. And that is before the feature-labeling work: even if an SAE successfully extracts millions of features, mapping those features to human-interpretable concepts requires either extensive manual inspection or automated LLM-based labeling at significant additional cost.

Beyond raw compute, structural problems emerge at scale. Dead latents—SAE hidden units that never activate—become more prevalent as dictionaries grow larger. The \(\ell_1\) penalty introduces bias toward solutions that may miss dense or nearly-dense feature directions. Features that genuinely require multi-dimensional representations (as Engels et al. demonstrate for circular variables) violate the monosemanticity assumption that justifies the whole approach and may land in the dictionary as multiple entangled units that resist straightforward interpretation.

Traditional circuit analysis faces a combinatorial version of the same wall. Identifying a circuit requires searching over model components and validating by ablation, each validation requiring multiple forward passes. For a 70B model with thousands of attention heads and MLP sublayers, exhaustive circuit search is computationally infeasible, and even targeted search—starting from a specific behavior—faces an exponentially growing state space as depth and width increase. Circuit results found in 7B models do not transfer reliably to 70B models; the internal organization may be qualitatively different, and there is currently no cheap way to check.

This is the situation that makes linear probes matter again, and not as a consolation. A linear probe on a 70B model costs a few GPU-hours: run a forward pass on tens of thousands of examples, extract activations, fit ridge regression. The probe dimension scales with model hidden dimension. With ridge, you do not need a full unregularized \(n > d\) sample; tens of thousands of examples is the usual working range, not a hard \(O(d)\) law. Probes are currently the only interpretability tool that runs at any model scale without a resource crisis.

There is a secondary argument that larger models may actually be easier to probe selectively. The superposition hypothesis predicts that as models grow, each feature occupies a more nearly-orthogonal direction in a higher-dimensional space, with less interference from other features. If so, probes on large models should be more selective—less of their accuracy should come from probe capacity and more from the clean linear accessibility of the target feature. This is a testable prediction, and preliminary evidence from the interpretability literature is roughly consistent with it: circuit analyses of large models often find cleaner, more interpretable structures than comparable analyses on smaller models.

For safety monitoring specifically, probes have no viable alternative at inference scale. A production system running hundreds of millions of forward passes per day cannot tolerate interpretability overhead that meaningfully degrades latency. A linear probe adds a single matrix-vector multiply and a threshold comparison. An SAE pass adds multiple matrix multiplications and a nonlinearity across a dictionary potentially orders of magnitude larger than the model’s hidden dimension. The cost difference is decisive, and Anthropic’s sleeper-agent probe work demonstrates that probes can provide meaningful safety signal at that cost level—not because probes answer all the right questions, but because they answer one important question cheaply enough to run continuously.

What Happens If We Fail to Build Better Tools

There is a version of the future where today’s mechanistic interpretability methods—SAEs, transcoders, circuit analysis—remain essentially unchanged while the models they need to analyze grow by another two or three orders of magnitude in capability. In that future, the mechanistic approaches become fully intractable at the frontier, and probes are what remain: the only tool cheap enough to run, providing real but limited signal about linear accessibility with no mechanistic depth at all.

This is not a comfortable outcome. Probes tell you whether a variable is linearly accessible; they do not reveal how it is computed, what drives it, or what the model will do in novel situations outside the probe’s training distribution. Using probes alone to understand models making consequential decisions is roughly equivalent to monitoring a complex system by asking whether an output thermometer reads high or low—the readout is informative, but the ignorance of the underlying mechanism is total.

The deeper risk is that AI interpretability becomes what computational neuroscience has been for the past sixty years: a field with excellent correlational tools, genuine scientific progress, and irreducible mechanistic opacity in the systems it most needs to explain. Neuroscience is in that position for reasons that are hard to work around. The brain cannot be fully accessed. Individual synapses cannot be read out at scale. Interventions are limited and imprecise. The gap between measurement and mechanistic understanding is large partly because of physical constraints that cannot be eliminated.

AI systems do not share those constraints. Neural networks run on hardware we control. The weights are fully readable. Every forward pass is exactly the reverse-mode differentiation of a function we defined. The mechanistic opacity of large neural networks—the fact that we cannot easily explain what a 70B parameter model is doing even though we built it—is an engineering problem, not a physical one. It reflects the absence of the right analytical tools, not the unavailability of the underlying data. That distinction matters because engineering problems are solvable.

Several directions point toward more tractable mechanistic tools. Hierarchical SAE training—finding coarse-grained features first and fine-grained features only for causally relevant categories—could reduce the computational cost of full-model feature discovery by orders of magnitude. Forward-model-free circuit discovery—using spectral analysis of weight matrices, gradient alignment, or singular value decompositions to infer circuit structure without exhaustive ablation—could make circuit analysis tractable at scales where per-component ablation is not. And computational neuroscience itself offers methods for analyzing high-dimensional dynamics that have not been fully applied to artificial networks: Gaussian process factor analysis, latent dynamical systems models, fixed-point finding in recurrent circuits. Some of these transfer directly and may reveal structure in sequential computation that SAE-style decomposition of static activations misses.

None of this is current infrastructure. It is a research agenda. But the agenda is real and the urgency grows with every parameter added to the models now being deployed. The goal of interpretability is to close the gap between having a complete description of a system’s computation in principle and being able to understand it in practice. Linear probes are useful in that project. They are a starting point, not a destination, and treating them as anything more permanent than that—as the stable floor that interpretability eventually rests on—is to accept a version of the neuroscience problem as the endpoint rather than as the condition we are building out of.

What Probes Precisely Establish

A linear probe, controlled against a selectivity baseline, establishes that variable \(y\) is linearly accessible from the network’s internal state at layer \(l\) under the training distribution, with the accuracy attributable to the representation quantified by the selectivity score. That is a meaningful property with theoretical motivation: it is the format that downstream layers can use without additional computational overhead, and gradient-based training reliably creates it for variables the network needs to use.

What it does not establish: that the variable is causally used (activation patching required), that the representation is geometrically clean (subspace analysis required), that layer \(l\) is where the variable is computed rather than where it has been passively propagated (circuit analysis required), or that the probe will remain accurate under distribution shifts that break the correlation structure between \(y\) and confounding variables in the hidden state.

The staged workflow proposed here—probe, stress-test residuals, intervene causally, characterize geometry, escalate to mechanistic analysis—provides a principled route from linear accessibility to mechanistic understanding wherever the cost of deeper analysis is justifiable. The workflow respects both what probes genuinely establish and what they leave open. And the question of how to make the deeper steps scalable to frontier models is the central open problem facing interpretability today.

The history of computational neuroscience shows what happens when you stop at the measurement tools that scale. You get a field with sixty years of rigorous science and persistent fundamental gaps. We have the opportunity to do better, because the systems we are studying are our own creation. The question is whether we build the tools to take advantage of that while it is still tractable.

A successful linear probe should not be read as “we have explained the system,” but it may legitimately be read as “the system has already done the work of making this variable operationally legible.” Getting that distinction right—and building the tools to go further—is the actual task.
